LECTURE 24

Feature Detection & Description — Part I

why corners · the SSD window measure · the structure matrix · the Harris corner detector
🧠 the big picture, first

Every earlier topic assumed we already had matched points across images. Now we find them automatically. The trick: pick points that are distinctive in every direction — and those turn out to be corners. Slide a little window; if the image changes no matter which way you nudge it, you've found a corner.

1 the matching problem

Given two views of a scene, we want corresponding points. Three stages:

🧩 detect → describe → match
  1. Detect repeatable landmark points (this lecture);
  2. Describe each with a distinctive signature of its neighbourhood;
  3. Match descriptors across images.

All three must survive transformations — translation, rotation, scale, illumination.

2 flat vs edge vs corner

Take a small window and shift it a little in various directions; watch how much the intensities change:

regionshift the window…change
flatany direction≈ no change
edgealong the edge / across itno change / big change
cornerany directionbig change every way ✅

3 the change measure & the structure matrix

Quantify "how much it changes" when we shift the window by $(u,v)$ with the sum of squared differences:

$$ E(u,v)=\sum_{(x,y)\in W}\big[\,I(x+u,\,y+v)-I(x,y)\,\big]^2 $$

For small shifts, first-order Taylor $I(x+u,y+v)\approx I+I_x u+I_y v$ turns $E$ into a neat quadratic form:

the structure (second-moment) matrix
$$ E(u,v)\approx\begin{bmatrix}u&v\end{bmatrix}H\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix},\quad H=\sum_{W}\begin{bmatrix}I_x^2 & I_xI_y\\ I_xI_y & I_y^2\end{bmatrix} $$
⚠️ average of squares, not square of average

$H$ sums the squared/product gradients over the window. If you instead squared the averaged gradient, $\det H$ would be $0$ everywhere — and corner detection would fail.

4 eigen-analysis — reading $H$

The eigenvectors of $H$ are the directions of largest and smallest change; the eigenvalues $\lambda_+\ge\lambda_-$ are the amounts of change:

the (λ₊, λ₋) map

flat: both small · edge: one large, one small · corner: both large.
read it as: a corner needs the smaller eigenvalue $\lambda_-$ to be large — that guarantees a big change even in the least-changing direction. So the corner score is $\min$ over all shift directions $=\lambda_-$.

see it on a chessboard — the whole point of $\lambda_-$

Run the eigen-analysis at every pixel of a chessboard and map the eigenvalues. This is the figure that makes the idea click:

Uniform squares → both eigenvalues ≈ 0 (black). Edges light up $\lambda_+$ but not $\lambda_-$. Only the corners light up $\lambda_-$ — which is exactly why $\lambda_-$ (not $\lambda_+$) is the corner score. (figure adapted from the course slides)

5 the detection algorithm

  1. Compute image gradients $I_x,I_y$ everywhere.
  2. At each pixel, build $H$ from windowed sums of $I_x^2,\,I_y^2,\,I_xI_y$.
  3. Compute the eigenvalues; keep points where $\lambda_-$ exceeds a threshold.
  4. Take the local maxima of the response → precise corner locations. 📍

(On a chessboard: $\lambda_+$ lights up edges and corners, but $\lambda_-$ lights up only the corners.)

6 the Harris measure (skip the square roots)

Computing eigenvalues needs a square root. Harris uses a cheaper response built from the determinant and trace instead:

Harris corner response
$$ f=\frac{\det H}{\operatorname{tr}H}=\frac{\lambda_+\lambda_-}{\lambda_++\lambda_-} $$

$\det H=\lambda_+\lambda_-$ (product), $\operatorname{tr}H=\lambda_++\lambda_-$ (sum) — no eigen-decomposition, no roots. Both eigenvalues large ⇒ $f$ large. Compute $f$ everywhere, take local maxima → the famous Harris corner detector.

📎 exam note — which "Harris" is this?

The measure taught here, $f=\dfrac{\det H}{\operatorname{tr}H}=\dfrac{\lambda_+\lambda_-}{\lambda_++\lambda_-}$, is the harmonic mean of the eigenvalues (the Noble/Brown variant). The original Harris–Stephens response is $R=\det H-k(\operatorname{tr}H)^2$ (with $k\approx0.04$–$0.06$), and Shi–Tomasi uses $\min(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)=\lambda_-$ directly. All three fire on corners; know all three names for the exam.

corners = points where the windowed gradient matrix $H$ has two large eigenvalues.

$E(u,v)\approx[u\,v]H[u\,v]^T$; corner score = $\lambda_-$; Harris uses $f=\det H/\operatorname{tr}H$ (no roots) + local maxima.

  1. Why are corners better feature points than edges or flat regions?
    a small window shift changes the intensity in every direction only at a corner — it's distinctive and localizable
  2. What do the two eigenvalues of the structure matrix $H$ tell you?
    the amount of intensity change in the most- and least-changing directions; a corner has both large
  3. Which eigenvalue is the corner score, and why?
    the smaller one $\lambda_-$ — it must be large so change is big even in the weakest direction
  4. What is the Harris response and why is it used instead of eigenvalues?
    $f=\det H/\operatorname{tr}H=\lambda_+\lambda_-/(\lambda_++\lambda_-)$ — it avoids square-root/eigen computation

LECTURE 25

Feature Detection & Description — Part II

scale invariance · the Gaussian pyramid · LoG & DoG · SIFT keypoints (edge culling & orientation)
🧠 the big picture, first

Harris finds corners but is scale-blind: shrink the image and its corners shift. The fix is to search for features not just over position but over scale too — a 3-D search $(x,y,\sigma)$. This is the heart of SIFT.

1 detection, description & the scale problem

Both detection and description must be invariant to transformation. Translation and rotation are relatively easy; scale is the hard one — a blob that looks like a corner up close may vanish when the image is shrunk. The idea: represent the image at many scales and find features that are strong at their own best scale.

2 multi-scale — the Gaussian pyramid

Repeatedly blur (Gaussian) and down-sample the image → a stack of ever-coarser versions, the Gaussian pyramid. A feature is now a local maximum in position AND scale (a 3-D search).

🎚️ octaves & the scale step $k$

The scales are grouped into octaves — an octave spans a doubling of $\sigma$ (scale factor $2$). If one octave holds $s{+}1$ blurred images, there are $s$ intervals, so adjacent scales differ by

$$ k=2^{1/s} $$

e.g. $10$ images per octave $\Rightarrow s=9 \Rightarrow k=2^{1/9}\approx 1.080$. (At the top of each octave the image is down-sampled by 2 and the next octave begins.)

blur + subsample → a pyramid

Each level: convolve with a Gaussian, then subsample.

3 the detector kernels — LoG & DoG

Start from the 2-D Gaussian (its width $\sigma$ = the "scale"):

$$ G(x,y,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{x^2+y^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) $$

Two blob-detecting measures, searched for extrema over scale:

scale-normalized Laplacian (LoG)
$$ \nabla^2_{\text{norm}}=\sigma^2\big(G_{xx}+G_{yy}\big) $$
Difference of Gaussians (DoG)
$$ \text{DoG}=G(x,y,k\sigma)-G(x,y,\sigma) $$

The $\sigma^2$ makes the Laplacian scale-invariant. And DoG is a cheap stand-in for LoG — because $\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial\sigma}=\sigma\nabla^2G$, so

$$ G(x,y,k\sigma)-G(x,y,\sigma)\approx (k-1)\,\sigma^2\nabla^2G $$

i.e. DoG $\propto$ scale-normalized Laplacian (the constant $(k-1)$ doesn't move the extrema). So you just subtract adjacent pyramid levels — no derivatives to compute.

4 SIFT keypoint detection

🔺 the two popular detectors
  • SIFT (David Lowe): build a DoG pyramid, then find extrema in the 3-D DoG scale-space (compare each sample to its neighbours in position and the scales above/below).
  • Harris–Laplacian: Harris corner response computed across a Laplacian pyramid; take maxima over scale & space.

5 culling bad keypoints (edges out)

The raw extrema include many weak/edge points. As in Harris, corners are robust but edges aren't. Use the Hessian of the DoG $H=\begin{bmatrix}D_{xx}&D_{xy}\\D_{xy}&D_{yy}\end{bmatrix}$ — its eigenvalues are the principal curvatures. An edge has one large, one small (very different) → reject via the trace/determinant ratio:

edge-rejection test
$$ \frac{(\operatorname{tr}H)^2}{\det H}<\frac{(r+1)^2}{r}\quad(r=10) $$

Keep a keypoint only if its curvatures are comparable (ratio below threshold); reject if $\dfrac{(\operatorname{tr}H)^2}{\det H}$ exceeds it. (E.g. 832 keypoints → 729 after a gradient threshold → fewer after this ratio test.)

6 orientation → rotation invariance

To be rotation-invariant, give each keypoint a canonical orientation: histogram the gradient directions in its neighbourhood (bins over $0$–$2\pi$), smooth it, and take the peak. Then describe everything relative to that direction.

so a SIFT keypoint carries: position + scale + orientation. (If the histogram has two strong peaks, the keypoint gets two descriptors.) Next lecture builds the descriptor itself.

search for features over position AND scale: DoG extrema in a Gaussian pyramid = SIFT keypoints.

DoG $\propto\sigma^2\nabla^2G$; cull edges by the Hessian ratio $(\operatorname{tr}H)^2/\det H$; a keypoint = position + scale + orientation.

  1. Why isn't the Harris detector scale-invariant, and what's the remedy?
    its window is one fixed size; search over a multi-scale (Gaussian pyramid) representation — find maxima in position and scale
  2. Why can SIFT use Difference-of-Gaussians instead of the Laplacian?
    DoG $\approx (k-1)\sigma^2\nabla^2G$ — it's proportional to the scale-normalized Laplacian, but only needs subtracting adjacent blurred levels
  3. How does SIFT throw out keypoints that lie on edges?
    via the DoG Hessian's principal curvatures: reject if $(\operatorname{tr}H)^2/\det H$ exceeds $(r+1)^2/r$ (one curvature ≫ the other = edge)
  4. What three things define a SIFT keypoint, and which gives rotation invariance?
    position, scale, and orientation; the orientation (peak of the gradient histogram) provides rotation invariance

LECTURE 26

Feature Detection & Description — Part III

the SIFT descriptor · SURF & integral images · fast binary methods: FAST, BRIEF, ORB
🧠 the big picture, first

We have keypoints (position + scale + orientation). Now give each a signature — a vector describing its neighbourhood — so we can match them. SIFT is the classic; then a family of faster descriptors (SURF, BRIEF, ORB) trade a little accuracy for real-time speed.

1 the SIFT descriptor (128 numbers)

Around a keypoint, take a $16\times16$ window, first rotated to the keypoint's canonical orientation (that's what makes it rotation-invariant). Then:

🧵 building the 128-D vector
  1. At each pixel compute the gradient; weak-magnitude ones are discarded.
  2. Split the $16\times16$ window into a $4\times4$ grid of cells (16 cells).
  3. Each cell → an 8-bin orientation histogram (votes weighted by gradient magnitude) = an 8-vector.
  4. Concatenate all 16 cells: $16\times8=\mathbf{128}$-dimensional descriptor. 🎯

SIFT (David Lowe, 1999) is robust to illumination and up to ~$60^\circ$ out-of-plane rotation.

2 SURF — speed via integral images

SURF (2006) approximates SIFT's expensive Gaussian work with box filters (weights $\pm1$) evaluated using an integral image:

integral image
$$ I_\Sigma(x,y)=\sum_{i\le x}\sum_{j\le y} I(i,j) $$
why it's fast: once $I_\Sigma$ is built in one scan, the sum over any rectangle is just $I_\Sigma$ at 4 corners (add two, subtract two) — 3 additions, 4 look-ups, independent of box size. So box-filter convolutions are $O(1)$ per pixel.
  • Detector: local maxima of the determinant of the Hessian, $\det H_{\text{approx}}=D_{xx}D_{yy}-(w\,D_{xy})^2$ ($w\approx0.9$), with box-filter approximations of the 2nd derivatives across scales.
  • Descriptor: Haar-wavelet responses in a $4\times4$ grid; each cell stores $(\sum d_x,\ \sum|d_x|,\ \sum d_y,\ \sum|d_y|)$ = 4 numbers → $16\times4=\mathbf{64}$-D.

3 fast binary methods — FAST, BRIEF, ORB

⚡ real-time detectors & descriptors
  • FAST (detector): look at 16 pixels on a circle around a candidate; if 12 consecutive are all brighter (or all darker) than the centre → corner. A trained decision tree (darker/similar/brighter) speeds it further. Not rotation/scale invariant.
  • BRIEF (descriptor): pick $n$ random point-pairs in the patch; each test $\tau=\mathbb 1[\,I(\mathbf p_i)binary string (128/256/512). Compared with Hamming distance — extremely fast. Not rotation invariant.
  • ORB = Oriented FAST + Rotated BRIEF: adds orientation via the intensity centroid (intensity-weighted centre gives a direction), then "steers" BRIEF to that angle + uses a scale pyramid. → rotation & scale aware, still fast.

4 the descriptor zoo, compared

methoddetectordescriptornote
SIFTDoG extrema128-D gradient histogramsgold standard; slow
SURFHessian det (box)64-D Haar responsesintegral image → fast
FAST/BRIEF12-point circle testbinary stringfastest; no rot/scale
ORBoriented FASTsteered/rotated BRIEFfast + rotation/scale

a descriptor turns a keypoint's neighbourhood into a matchable vector — SIFT (128-D) for accuracy, ORB for speed.

SIFT = 4×4 cells × 8-bin gradient histograms; SURF uses integral images + box filters; BRIEF/ORB = binary strings via intensity tests.

  1. How does the 128-dimensional SIFT descriptor arise?
    an orientation-corrected $16\times16$ window → $4\times4$ cells × 8-bin gradient-orientation histograms = $16\times8=128$
  2. What makes SURF fast, and how does an integral image help?
    box filters instead of Gaussians; an integral image gives any rectangle's sum in 3 add/4 look-ups regardless of size
  3. What is the FAST corner test?
    on 16 pixels around a circle, ≥12 consecutive brighter-or-darker than the centre ⇒ a corner
  4. How does ORB make FAST+BRIEF rotation-invariant?
    it computes an orientation from the intensity centroid and steers (rotates) the BRIEF sampling pattern to it

LECTURE 27

Feature Detection & Description — Part IV

matching by distance · region descriptors: HOG · texture: LBP, co-occurrence matrix, Laws energy
🧠 the big picture, first

Once every keypoint is a vector, matching is just "find the nearest vector." Then we zoom out from points to regions: how to describe a whole patch or texture (HOG for shapes, LBP / co-occurrence / Laws for textures) — the descriptors behind object and material recognition.

1 matching by distance

Two keypoints match if their descriptors are close. Measure closeness with a norm between feature vectors $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$:

$$ L_1=\sum_i|u_i-v_i|,\quad L_2=\sqrt{\sum_i (u_i-v_i)^2},\quad L_p=\Big(\sum_i |u_i-v_i|^p\Big)^{1/p} $$

($L_1$ = Manhattan, $L_2$ = Euclidean; both special cases of $L_p$.) The nearest descriptor is the candidate match. Non-maximal suppression then removes duplicate/overlapping detections greedily: keep the best-scoring window, suppress those too close, repeat.

2 region descriptor — Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG)

HOG (2005) describes a whole patch by its gradient-orientation statistics — great for shape (famously, pedestrian detection):

📐 HOG recipe (for a 64×128 patch)
  1. Compute gradients (no smoothing); for colour, use the channel with the largest gradient.
  2. Divide into $16\times16$ blocks with 50% overlap → ~105 blocks; each block = $2\times2$ cells of $8\times8$.
  3. Per cell: a 9-bin orientation histogram, votes weighted by magnitude (interpolated, Gaussian-weighted).
  4. Concatenate: $105\times4\times9=\mathbf{3780}$-D descriptor → feed a classifier (SVM / random forest).

3 texture — first, what is it?

Texture is a spatial arrangement of intensities/colours in a region — a quantitative measure of that arrangement distinctly identifies a material (sky vs road vs foliage). The professor lists four texture techniques: edge density & direction, LBP, co-occurrence matrix, and Laws energy.

method 1 — edge density & direction

Compute the gradient at every pixel in the region, then build two normalized histograms — one of gradient magnitudes, one of gradient directions (normalized so each histogram's area = 1, i.e. a probability density; typically a small number of bins, ~10). The concatenated pair is the texture descriptor; compare textures with an $L_1$/$L_2$ distance.

4 texture descriptor — Local Binary Pattern (LBP)

Compare a central pixel $c$ to its 8 neighbours; each is a bit (1 if brighter). Read the 8 bits as a number:

local binary pattern
$$ \text{LBP}(c)=\sum_{i=0}^{7} b(i)\,2^{i},\qquad b(i)=\begin{cases}1 & I_i\ge I_c\\ 0 & \text{else}\end{cases} $$

So each pixel gets a value $0$–$255$; a normalized histogram over the region is the texture descriptor. It's invariant to monotonic illumination change, but not rotation.

🔄 rotation-invariant & uniform LBP
  • Use a circular neighbourhood ($P$ points, radius $R$; interpolate off-grid points). For rotation invariance, take the minimum over all circular bit-shifts (rotating the image just rotates the string).
  • Uniform patterns have $\le 2$ bit-transitions (e.g. 11100011); for $P=8$ there are 9 uniform patterns → a compact 10-bin histogram (9 uniform + 1 "all others"). Add local variance for intensity contrast.

5 texture descriptor — co-occurrence matrix

Count how often two grey levels appear at a fixed spatial offset $\mathbf t=(a,b)$: the $(x,y)$ entry is the number of pixel pairs with values $x$ and $y$ at that relation.

$$ C_{\mathbf t}(x,y)=\#\{\, \mathbf p:\ I(\mathbf p)=x\ \text{and}\ I(\mathbf p+\mathbf t)=y\,\} $$
worked example — a 2-level 4×4 image, offset "one step right"

Counting each ordered pair $(x,y)$ across the image gave the professor:

$$ C=\begin{bmatrix}4 & 2\\ 2 & 4\end{bmatrix} $$

i.e. $(0,0)$ occurred 4×, $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ twice each, $(1,1)$ 4×. Normalize (divide by the total) → a probability matrix; from it derive features like correlation, contrast, energy, homogeneity. Different offsets → different matrices → a richer texture signature.

6 texture descriptor — Laws energy

Laws' texture energy builds masks from four 1-D filters — L5 (level/average), E5 (edge), S5 (spot), R5 (ripple):

  • Take outer products of pairs (e.g. $\mathbf{E5}\,\mathbf{L5}^{T}$) → $5\times5$ 2-D masks; combine symmetric pairs down to 9 masks.
  • Convolve, then compute energy = sum of squared/absolute responses in a window (e.g. $15\times15$) → a 9-D feature per pixel.

All these region descriptors feed the same downstream task: match to a labelled library or train a classifier (segmentation, object/material recognition).

match points by nearest descriptor; describe regions by gradient/texture statistics (HOG, LBP, co-occurrence, Laws).

$L_1/L_2$ distances + non-max suppression; HOG = 9-bin gradient histograms; LBP = thresholded-neighbour bit string; co-occurrence counts grey-level pairs.

  1. Two SIFT descriptors — how do you decide if they match, and what removes duplicate detections?
    nearest descriptor by an $L_1$/$L_2$ distance; non-maximal suppression removes overlapping duplicates
  2. What does HOG capture and what is a classic application?
    gradient-orientation histograms over a grid (shape); pedestrian detection
  3. Write the LBP value formula and state one invariance it lacks.
    $\text{LBP}=\sum_i b(i)2^i$ (bit = neighbour ≥ centre); it is not rotation-invariant (fixed by circular LBP + min-over-shifts)
  4. What does a grey-level co-occurrence matrix count?
    how often each pair of grey values $(x,y)$ occurs at a fixed spatial offset $\mathbf t$

LECTURE 28

Feature Detection & Description — Part V

describing a whole image · Bag of Visual Words · VLAD · content-based image retrieval
🧠 the big picture, first

We've described points and regions; now the whole image in one vector — so you can search by picture. The trick borrows from text search: build a dictionary of "visual words" and describe any image by which visual words it contains.

1 Bag of Visual Words (BoVW)

Text documents are described by which dictionary words they contain. Images have no ready-made dictionary — so we build one from local descriptors:

📖 building & using the visual dictionary
  1. Over a library of images, extract keypoints (SIFT/SURF/FAST) and their descriptors → a huge pile of vectors.
  2. Cluster them with K-means into $K$ groups; each cluster mean is a visual word. The $K$ words are the codebook.
  3. For any image: describe each keypoint, assign it to its nearest visual word, and count → a $K$-bin histogram.

Every image becomes a fixed $K$-dimensional vector, regardless of how many keypoints it had. 🎯

2 VLAD — remember the residuals

BoVW only counts words. VLAD (Vector of Locally Aggregated Descriptors) keeps more: for each visual word $C_i$, it sums the differences between the descriptors assigned to it and the centre:

VLAD aggregation
$$ \mathbf v_i=\!\!\sum_{\text{NN}(\mathbf x)=C_i}\!\!(\mathbf x-C_i) $$

Concatenate all $K$ residual vectors (each of the descriptor dimension $D$, e.g. $D=128$ for SIFT) → a $K\times D$ vector, then L2-normalize:

$$ \text{VLAD}=\frac{[\,\mathbf v_1;\mathbf v_2;\dots;\mathbf v_K\,]}{\big\lVert[\,\mathbf v_1;\dots;\mathbf v_K\,]\big\rVert} $$

It's higher-dimensional than BoVW (so $K$ is kept small), but more discriminative — it records where descriptors sit relative to each word, not just how many.

3 application — content-based image retrieval

A global descriptor lets you search by image: describe every library image; given a query image, compute its descriptor and return the library images whose descriptors are closest, ranked.

in practice: "photograph this temple carving, find what it is." (The professor's examples: the chariot at Hampi's Vithala temple and the Bishnupur terracotta temples.) Occasional wrong matches happen because different images can land on similar feature vectors.

4 look-ahead — matching & robust model fitting

These tools belong to the next topic (matching & model fitting) but are the natural sequel to descriptors — and show up in the assignment, so know them:

toolwhat it does
RANSACRobust model fitting with outliers: randomly sample a minimal set, fit a model, count inliers (points within a threshold), repeat, keep the model with the most inliers. Tolerates a large fraction of wrong matches — the standard way to fit $F$/$H$ from noisy correspondences.
Hough transformVoting-based line/shape fitting: each point votes for all lines through it in parameter space; peaks in the accumulator = detected lines. Strong when there are many lines / much clutter.
K-D treeFast nearest-neighbour search for matching descriptors: a binary space-partition tree; average query $\approx O(\log n)$ (grows with dimension $d$). Speeds up "find the closest descriptor."
KL divergenceA statistical distance between two probability distributions $D_{KL}(P\Vert Q)=\sum_i P_i\log\tfrac{P_i}{Q_i}$ — used to compare histograms/distributions. Not a geometric line-fitting criterion (a common distractor).
