LECTURE 11

Camera Geometry — Part I

the pinhole model · perspective projection as a matrix · building the 3×4 camera matrix P = K[R|t]
🧠 the big picture, first

A camera is a machine that flattens a 3-D world onto a 2-D image. This lecture writes that flattening as a single matrix multiply: $\mathbf x = P\mathbf X$, where $P$ is a $3\times4$ matrix built from what the camera is ($K$) and where it sits ($R,\mathbf t$).

Get $P$ and you can predict exactly where any world point lands in the photo. The catch: you can't run it backwards (a pixel came from a whole ray, not one point). 📸

1 the pinhole camera & perspective projection

Put the camera centre (centre of projection) at the origin. A world point $P=(X,Y,Z)$ sends a ray through the centre; where that ray hits the image plane (a distance $f$ = the focal length away) is the image point $(x,y)$. By similar triangles:

perspective projection
$$ x=f\,\frac{X}{Z},\qquad y=f\,\frac{Y}{Z} $$

similar triangles give x = fX/Z

Big triangle (O, P, its foot) ∼ small triangle (O, p, its foot) ⇒ $x/f = X/Z$.
💡 why is the image plane drawn in front of the centre?

Physically the sensor sits behind the lens/centre, so the true image forms inverted (upside-down and left-right flipped). To avoid dragging that sign flip through every formula, we place a virtual image plane in front of the centre at the same distance $f$. It carries an upright image of exactly the same size, and the geometry is identical (same similar triangles) — so all the equations come out clean. That convention is why every diagram here shows the plane in front. 🙃➡️🙂

Names to know: the $XY$-plane of the camera frame is the principal plane; the $Z$-axis is the principal (optical) axis; the axis pierces the image plane at the principal point. That $\div Z$ is why far things look small — perspective foreshortening.

2 writing it as a matrix — the 3×4 projection matrix

The division by $Z$ is non-linear… until we use homogeneous coordinates. Represent the world point as a 4-vector $(X,Y,Z,1)^{T}$ and the image point as a 3-vector; then projection is linear:

$$ \begin{bmatrix}fX\\fY\\Z\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f&0&0&0\\0&f&0&0\\0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix} $$
read it as: the output $(fX,fY,Z)$ is a homogeneous image point — divide by the last entry $Z$ and you recover $(fX/Z,\,fY/Z)$, exactly our projection. The scale factor is the depth $Z$.

That $3\times4$ matrix is the projection matrix $P$. In canonical form it splits as:

$$ P=\operatorname{diag}(f,f,1)\,[\,I\mid \mathbf 0\,] $$

Pulling $f$ out leaves the bare $[\,I\mid\mathbf 0\,]$ — the minimal / canonical camera. This is precisely the camera you get when the image plane is normalised to unit distance ($f=1$) from the centre. Everything else ($f$, the principal point, pixels, skew) is just extra structure layered on top of this canonical core.

dimension check: this is a linear map from the 3-D projective space $\mathbb P^3$ (world points $(X,Y,Z,1)^{T}$) down to the 2-D projective space $\mathbb P^2$ (image points $(x,y,1)^{T}$) — written $\mathbb P^3\!\to\!\mathbb P^2$. Contrast a homography, which is $\mathbb P^2\!\to\!\mathbb P^2$ (plane→plane, square and invertible). The drop in dimension is why depth is lost.
⚠️ 3-D→2-D is NOT invertible

Unlike a homography (a square, invertible $3\times3$ map between two planes), $P$ is $3\times4$ — not square. A 3-D point maps to a unique pixel, but a pixel maps back to an entire ray of 3-D points. You cannot recover depth from one image. 🚫

3 shifting the origin — the principal point

Real image coordinates start from a corner, not the principal point. If the principal point sits at $(p_x,p_y)$, every image coordinate is offset: $x=f\tfrac{X}{Z}+p_x$, $y=f\tfrac{Y}{Z}+p_y$. That offset moves into the third column, and the focal/offset part becomes the calibration matrix $K$:

calibration matrix (so far)
$$ K=\begin{bmatrix}f&0&p_x\\0&f&p_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf x=K\,[\,I\mid\mathbf 0\,]\,\mathbf X $$
check the offset by hand — where does the $+p_x$ come from?
  1. The third column of $P$ changes from $(0,0,1)^{T}$ to $(p_x,p_y,1)^{T}$. Multiply out: $\begin{bmatrix}f&0&p_x&0\\0&f&p_y&0\\0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}fX+p_xZ\\ fY+p_yZ\\ Z\end{bmatrix}$.
  2. Dehomogenise (divide by the last entry $Z$): $\ x'=\dfrac{fX+p_xZ}{Z}=f\dfrac{X}{Z}+p_x$, and likewise $\ y'=f\dfrac{Y}{Z}+p_y$. ✔️ — exactly the shifted coordinates we wanted.

4 the camera isn't at the origin — extrinsics R, t

Usually the world has its own frame, offset and rotated from the camera's. A world point $\tilde{\mathbf X}$ converts to camera coordinates by a rigid motion: rotate + translate.

$$ \tilde{\mathbf X}_{\text{cam}}=R\big(\tilde{\mathbf X}-\tilde{\mathbf C}\big) $$

$R$ = $3\times3$ rotation (orthonormal) matrix; $\tilde{\mathbf C}$ = the camera centre's position in world coordinates. (Tilde = ordinary non-homogeneous coordinates.)

assemble $P$ step-by-step — the $4\times4$ world→camera block
  1. Write the rigid motion in homogeneous form as one $4\times4$ matrix: $\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf X}_{\text{cam}}\\ 1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R & -R\tilde{\mathbf C}\\ \mathbf 0^{T} & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf X}\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$.
  2. Cascade it with the canonical camera $K[\,I\mid\mathbf 0\,]$: $\quad \mathbf x = K\,[\,I\mid\mathbf 0\,]\begin{bmatrix}R & -R\tilde{\mathbf C}\\ \mathbf 0^{T} & 1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf X$.
  3. Count the shapes: $\underbrace{K}_{3\times3}\cdot\underbrace{[\,I\mid\mathbf 0\,]}_{3\times4}\cdot\underbrace{\text{block}}_{4\times4}$ collapses into a single $3\times4$ matrix — and that product is $P$. (Multiplying the first two already gives $K[\,R\mid -R\tilde{\mathbf C}\,]$.)

Fold this into the projection — the full projective camera:

the full camera matrix
$$ P=K\,[\,R\mid \mathbf t\,]=K\,R\,[\,I\mid -\tilde{\mathbf C}\,],\qquad \mathbf t=-R\tilde{\mathbf C} $$
read it as: "un-rotate & un-shift the world into the camera's frame ($[R\mid\mathbf t]$), then apply the lens ($K$)." Handy consequence: the camera centre is recoverable straight from $P$, since the $-\tilde{\mathbf C}$ sits right there.

5 real sensors — pixels & skew

A digital sensor (CCD/CMOS) has a finite pixel density. If there are $m_x$ pixels per unit length horizontally and $m_y$ vertically, the focal length in pixels becomes $\alpha_x=f\,m_x$ and $\alpha_y=f\,m_y$ (they differ if pixels aren't square). Add a skew $s$ for sensors whose axes aren't exactly perpendicular:

the full calibration matrix
$$ K=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_x & s & p_x\\ 0 & \alpha_y & p_y\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} $$

$s\approx \alpha_x\tan\theta$ for a tiny deviation $\theta$ from 90°; on modern cameras $s\approx0$. $K$ is upper-triangular with 5 free numbers.

6 counting the parameters — 11 DOF

The $3\times4$ $P$ has 12 entries, but it's defined only up to scale ⇒ 11 degrees of freedom, split into two groups:

groupparameterscount
Intrinsic (inside $K$)$\alpha_x,\ \alpha_y,\ s,\ p_x,\ p_y$5
Extrinsic (in $[R\mid\mathbf t]$)3 rotation + 3 translation6

Intrinsics describe the camera itself (lens + sensor); extrinsics describe its pose in the world. Together: $P=K[R\mid\mathbf t]$, $\ \mathbf x=P\mathbf X$.

a camera = one 3×4 matrix $P=K[R\,|\,\mathbf t]$: lens & sensor ($K$) times pose ($R,\mathbf t$).

perspective divides by depth; projection is linear in homogeneous coords; 11 DOF = 5 intrinsic + 6 extrinsic; not invertible.

  1. Why does a distant object appear smaller in a photo?
    perspective projection divides by depth: $x=fX/Z$, so larger $Z$ shrinks the image coordinate
  2. Why can't you recover a 3-D point from its single image point?
    $P$ is $3\times4$ (not invertible); a pixel corresponds to a whole ray of 3-D points — depth is lost
  3. What do the 5 intrinsic parameters in $K$ represent?
    focal lengths in pixels $\alpha_x,\alpha_y$, skew $s$, and principal point $p_x,p_y$
  4. Write the full camera matrix in terms of $K$, $R$ and the camera centre $\tilde{\mathbf C}$.
    $P=K R\,[\,I\mid-\tilde{\mathbf C}\,]=K[\,R\mid\mathbf t\,]$ with $\mathbf t=-R\tilde{\mathbf C}$
  5. Why is the image plane drawn in front of the camera centre?
    it's a virtual plane at the same distance $f$; the real image behind the lens is inverted, so the front plane gives an upright, same-size image with identical geometry

LECTURE 12

Camera Geometry — Part II

intrinsic parameters · the calibration matrix K · pixels, principal point & skew
🧠 the big picture, first

Last lecture we said a camera is a machine that turns a 3-D world point $\mathbf X$ into a 2-D image point $\mathbf x$ by a single matrix multiply, $\mathbf x = P\mathbf X$. Today we open up that matrix $P$ and see what is inside.

$P$ splits cleanly into two halves: where the camera sits and points (the extrinsic part, $R$ and the centre) and how the camera turns metres on the focal plane into pixels (the intrinsic part, the calibration matrix $K$). We will also learn how to recover $P$ from a handful of point matches, and read the camera centre and vanishing points straight off it. 📷

1 recap — the projective camera $P$

A projective camera is a $3\times4$ matrix $P$ that maps a world point to an image point in homogeneous (projective) coordinates:

$$ \mathbf x = P\,\mathbf X,\qquad \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\w\end{bmatrix}\ (3\text{-vector}),\quad \mathbf X=\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix}\ (4\text{-vector}) $$
read it as: “stick a $1$ onto the 3-D point to make it a 4-vector, hit it with $P$, and out drops a 3-vector image point.” The image point is homogeneous: divide by its last entry $w$ to read off the real pixel $\big(x/w,\ y/w\big)$.

The professor reminds us that $P$ is not one solid block — it factors into recognisable pieces:

the camera, factored
$$ P = K\,[\,R \mid \mathbf t\,] = K\,R\,[\,I \mid -\tilde{\mathbf C}\,] $$
🧩 what each factor does
  • $R$ — a $3\times3$ rotation matrix. It rotates the world axes until they line up with the camera's own axes (a change of orientation). Extrinsic.
  • $\mathbf t$ / $\tilde{\mathbf C}$ — the translation that moves the world origin onto the camera centre $\tilde{\mathbf C}$. Extrinsic.
  • $K$ — the calibration matrix: it converts the tidy metric image on the focal plane into actual pixel indices. Intrinsic. This is today's star.

the two coordinate frames: world vs camera

Extrinsics ($R,\mathbf t$) carry you from the world frame to the camera-centred frame; intrinsics ($K$) then take over inside the camera.

2 the calibration matrix $K$ — intrinsic parameters

Intrinsic (internal) parameters are the ones baked into the camera hardware itself — the lens focal length, the sensor's pixel size, where the optical axis lands on the chip. They do not change when you move the camera around. All of them live in one upper-triangular $3\times3$ matrix:

the calibration matrix
$$ K=\begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & p_x\\[2pt] 0 & f_y & p_y\\[2pt] 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
read it as: “scale the two axes by $f_x,f_y$, shear them by the skew $s$, then shift the origin to the principal point $(p_x,p_y)$.” Three simple 2-D operations — scaling, shearing, translating — stacked into one matrix.

step 1 — from an ideal image to pixels

Imagine the perfect, textbook image formed on the focal plane, measured in millimetres with its origin on the optical axis. A camera-frame point $(X,Y,Z)$ lands there at the ideal coordinates $\big(f\tfrac{X}{Z},\ f\tfrac{Y}{Z}\big)$, where $f$ is the focal length in mm. But a sensor doesn't count millimetres — it counts pixels. Two fixes are needed.

🔧 the two fixes that build $K$
  1. Scale mm → pixels. Let $m_x$ = pixels per mm along the horizontal, $m_y$ = pixels per mm along the vertical. Then the focal length expressed in pixels along each axis is
    $$ f_x = f\,m_x,\qquad f_y = f\,m_y $$
    These $f_x,f_y$ are the resolution factors — the same $f$ measured in two different pixel rulers. (Lecture wording: the professor calls $m_x,m_y$ the “number of pixels along the horizontal / vertical direction”. Read that as the pixel density — pixels per unit length — which is the standard meaning used here, so $f\,m$ correctly comes out in pixels.)
  2. Shift the origin. Pixel indices are counted from a corner of the sensor, not from the optical axis. So we add the principal point $(p_x,p_y)$ — the pixel where the optical axis actually pierces the image (usually near the centre of the frame).

Putting both together, the pixel coordinates $(u,v)$ of a camera-frame point are:

$$ u = f_x\,\frac{X}{Z} + s\,\frac{Y}{Z} + p_x,\qquad v = f_y\,\frac{Y}{Z} + p_y $$
read it as: “take the ideal projection, magnify it by the pixels-per-unit resolution, nudge in a pinch of skew, and slide the origin over to the principal point.” That is exactly multiplying by $K$.

ideal metric image → pixel array (what $K$ does)

Left: pixel indices start at a corner; the metric image sits centred on the principal point. Right: skew tilts one axis. $K$ encodes all of this.

the five knobs inside $K$

entrynamewhat it means
$f_x=f\,m_x$focal length in pixels (x)how many pixels one unit of horizontal focal length spans
$f_y=f\,m_y$focal length in pixels (y)same, vertically; differs from $f_x$ if pixels aren't square
$p_x$principal point (x)column index where the optical axis hits the sensor
$p_y$principal point (y)row index where the optical axis hits the sensor
$s$skewtilt between the pixel axes; $s=0$ for an ideal rectangular grid

That is 5 intrinsic parameters — remember this count, it comes back in Section 5.

aspect ratio, the determinant & why $K$ is upper-triangular

📏 aspect ratio

The ratio $\dfrac{f_y}{f_x}=\dfrac{m_y}{m_x}$ is the aspect ratio of a pixel. Square pixels ⇒ $m_x=m_y$ ⇒ ratio $=1$ ⇒ $f_x=f_y$.

🧮 determinant of $K$

Because $K$ is triangular, its determinant is just the product of the diagonal:

$$ \det K = f_x\,f_y $$

the professor's “product of the two resolution factors”.

▲ why upper-triangular?

Going from the ideal image to pixels only ever scales the axes, shears them, and offsets the origin — none of those operations feed the last (homogeneous) coordinate back into the first two rows. Every such 2-D affine map has zeros below the diagonal, so their product $K$ is upper-triangular. (Formally, $K$ falls out of the RQ decomposition of the left $3\times3$ block of $P$, which is defined to give an upper-triangular $K$ and an orthonormal $R$.)

projection for a camera at the origin

If the camera sits at the world origin looking straight down its axis (no rotation, no translation, so $R=I$ and $\tilde{\mathbf C}=\mathbf 0$), the whole camera collapses to just its intrinsics:

canonical projection (camera at origin)
$$ \mathbf x = K\,[\,I \mid \mathbf 0\,]\,\mathbf X $$
read it as: “$[\,I\mid\mathbf 0\,]$ just chops the homogeneous $1$ off $\mathbf X$ to give $(X,Y,Z)$; then $K$ turns that into a pixel.” It's the simplest possible camera — pure intrinsics.
worked example — project one point through $K$ (illustrative numbers)

Let $f_x=f_y=800$ px, principal point $(p_x,p_y)=(320,240)$, skew $s=0$, camera at the origin. A point in front of the camera has camera-frame coordinates $(X,Y,Z)=(0.2,\,0.1,\,2)$.

  1. $K[I\mid\mathbf 0]\mathbf X = K\,(0.2,\ 0.1,\ 2)^{\top} = \big(800(0.2)+320(2),\ \ 800(0.1)+240(2),\ \ 2\big) = (800,\ 560,\ 2)$.
  2. Dehomogenise (divide by $w=2$): pixel $=(800/2,\ 560/2)=(400,\ 280)$.
  3. Check with the shortcut: $u=f_x\tfrac{X}{Z}+p_x=800(0.1)+320=400$; $\ v=f_y\tfrac{Y}{Z}+p_y=800(0.05)+240=280$. ✔️

With a non-zero skew $s$, step 1's first entry would gain a term $s\cdot Y = s(0.1)$ — a small horizontal shear.

3 $K^{-1}$ and normalized (canonical) coordinates

Since $K$ is upper-triangular, so is its inverse $K^{-1}$. Multiplying a pixel by $K^{-1}$ undoes the camera's internal calibration:

$$ \hat{\mathbf x} = K^{-1}\mathbf x $$
🎯 what $K^{-1}\mathbf x$ gives you

It returns the point's normalized (canonical) image coordinates: the ideal image you'd get from a camera whose focal plane sits at unit distance ($f=1$) and whose axes are parallel to the camera-centred coordinate axes. In other words, you strip away the pixel scaling, the principal-point offset and the skew, leaving pure direction information.

intuition: $K$ is the “make it look like this particular sensor” step; $K^{-1}$ is the “forget which sensor took it” step. Normalized coordinates let different cameras speak the same geometric language.

4 slicing $P$ into blocks: $M$ and $\mathbf p_4$

The professor introduces the working notation. Write the $3\times4$ matrix $P$ as its left $3\times3$ block $M$ plus its last column $\mathbf p_4$:

$$ P = [\,M \mid \mathbf p_4\,],\qquad M = K R \quad(\text{a }3\times3\text{ block}),\qquad \mathbf p_4 = \text{4th column}\ (3\times1) $$

Factor $M$ back out and you reach the canonical form of the camera:

$$ P = [\,M \mid \mathbf p_4\,] = M\,[\,I \mid M^{-1}\mathbf p_4\,] = M\,[\,I \mid -\tilde{\mathbf C}\,] $$
read it as: “pull $M$ out front, and the leftover last column becomes $-\tilde{\mathbf C}$.” Matching the two forms gives a free formula for the camera centre: $\tilde{\mathbf C} = -M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$.

Two useful decompositions of the same $P$: $\ [\,M\mid\mathbf p_4\,]$ (block + column) and $\ M[\,I\mid-\tilde{\mathbf C}\,]$ (canonical). $M$ itself further splits as $M=KR$ — intrinsics times rotation.

5 properties of the projective camera

📋 the facts to memorise
  • Size & rank. $P$ is $3\times4$ and has rank 3 (full row rank).
  • Degrees of freedom = 11. $P$ has $12$ entries but is defined only up to scale (multiply the whole matrix by any non-zero constant and it's the same camera), so one degree is spent — leaving $11$ independent parameters.
  • $11 = 6 + 5$. Six extrinsic parameters (3 for the rotation $R$ + 3 for the translation of the origin) and five intrinsic parameters (the $f_x,f_y,s,p_x,p_y$ inside $K$).
$$ \underbrace{11}_{\text{DOF of }P} \;=\; \underbrace{6}_{\text{extrinsic }(R,\ \mathbf t)} \;+\; \underbrace{5}_{\text{intrinsic }(K)} $$

6 from one match to two equations (the cross-product trick)

Suppose experimentally we know a 3-D scene point $\mathbf X_i$ and its observed image point $\mathbf x_i=(x_i,\,y_i,\,w_i)^{\top}$. We'd like to use this pair to pin down $P$. The catch: because we're in projective space, $\mathbf x_i$ and $P\mathbf X_i$ are only equal up to an unknown scale factor $\lambda$, so we cannot just set their entries equal:

$$ \mathbf x_i \sim P\mathbf X_i \qquad(\text{equal in direction, not in length}) $$
✖️ kill the scale with a cross product

Two vectors that point the same direction (differ only by a scale) have a zero cross product. So the scale factor simply vanishes if we write:

$$ \mathbf x_i \times (P\mathbf X_i) = \mathbf 0 $$

Write $P$ as three row vectors $\mathbf r_1^{\top},\mathbf r_2^{\top},\mathbf r_3^{\top}$ (each $1\times4$). Then $P\mathbf X_i$ is a column of three scalars, and the cross product with $\mathbf x_i$ expands into three equations:

$$ P\mathbf X_i=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf r_1^{\top}\mathbf X_i\\ \mathbf r_2^{\top}\mathbf X_i\\ \mathbf r_3^{\top}\mathbf X_i\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf x_i \times P\mathbf X_i=\begin{bmatrix} y_i\,\mathbf r_3^{\top}\mathbf X_i - w_i\,\mathbf r_2^{\top}\mathbf X_i\\[2pt] w_i\,\mathbf r_1^{\top}\mathbf X_i - x_i\,\mathbf r_3^{\top}\mathbf X_i\\[2pt] x_i\,\mathbf r_2^{\top}\mathbf X_i - y_i\,\mathbf r_1^{\top}\mathbf X_i \end{bmatrix}=\mathbf 0 $$

Regrouping so the unknown rows $\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_3$ stand on the right gives the tidy matrix form (note each block is a $1\times4$ row, $\mathbf 0^{\top}$ is a $1\times4$ zero):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf 0^{\top} & -w_i\mathbf X_i^{\top} & \ \ y_i\mathbf X_i^{\top}\\[2pt] w_i\mathbf X_i^{\top} & \mathbf 0^{\top} & -x_i\mathbf X_i^{\top}\\[2pt] -y_i\mathbf X_i^{\top} & \ \ x_i\mathbf X_i^{\top} & \mathbf 0^{\top} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf r_1\\ \mathbf r_2\\ \mathbf r_3\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf 0 $$
⚠️ only two of the three are independent

Multiply row 1 by $x_i$, row 2 by $y_i$, add them — you recover row 3 (times $w_i$). So the third equation is redundant: each point correspondence supplies exactly 2 independent equations.

so, the headcount: $P$ has 11 unknowns; each match gives 2 equations. You need at least $\lceil 11/2\rceil = 6$ point correspondences (giving $12$ equations) to solve for the camera. 🎯

7 estimating $P$ — the Direct Linear Transform (DLT)

Collect $n$ correspondences. Each contributes $2$ rows, so stacking them gives a tall matrix $A$ of size $2n\times12$ acting on the $12$-vector $\mathbf p$ (all the entries of $P$, read out row by row):

$$ A\,\mathbf p = \mathbf 0,\qquad A\in\mathbb R^{\,2n\times12},\quad \mathbf p=\big(\mathbf r_1;\,\mathbf r_2;\,\mathbf r_3\big)\in\mathbb R^{12} $$

With $n\ge6$ we have more equations than unknowns (an over-determined homogeneous system), so we solve it in the least-squares sense. Since any scaled $\mathbf p$ is also a solution, we pin the scale with the constraint $\lVert\mathbf p\rVert=1$:

DLT objective
$$ \min_{\mathbf p}\ \lVert A\mathbf p\rVert \quad\text{subject to}\quad \lVert\mathbf p\rVert = 1 $$
🛠️ how to actually solve it
  • Eigenvector route: the minimiser is the eigenvector of $A^{\top}A$ with the smallest eigenvalue (equivalently, the last right-singular vector of $A$). Reshape that 12-vector back into $P$.
  • Non-homogeneous route: fix one element of $\mathbf p$ to a value and solve the remaining ordinary least-squares system.
  • This is the very same Direct Linear Transform machinery used earlier to estimate a homography — identical recipe, bigger matrix.

8 reading geometry straight off $P$

the camera centre is $P$'s null-space

The camera centre $\tilde{\mathbf C}$ is a singular point: you cannot photograph the very point light rays pass through — there is no ray joining a point to itself. In projective terms the homogeneous centre $\mathbf C=(\tilde{\mathbf C};1)$ maps to the zero vector:

$$ P\,\mathbf C = \mathbf 0 $$

Split $P=[\,M\mid\mathbf p_4\,]$ and multiply out: $\ M\tilde{\mathbf C}+\mathbf p_4=\mathbf 0$, which rearranges to the formula we already glimpsed:

camera centre (finite camera)
$$ \tilde{\mathbf C} = -\,M^{-1}\,\mathbf p_4 $$
read it as: “grab the left $3\times3$ block $M$, invert it, and multiply the last column — negated — to get where the camera is.” Valid whenever $M$ is invertible.
🔎 finite vs. camera-at-infinity

For a finite camera $M$ is non-singular and the formula above works. For a camera at infinity the centre is itself a point at infinity, so its homogeneous scale factor is $0$ and $M$ turns out singular ($M^{-1}$ does not exist, so $-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$ is unavailable). The professor writes the centre directly in homogeneous form as the null-direction of $M$:

$$ \mathbf C=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf d\\[2pt] 0\end{bmatrix},\qquad M\,\mathbf d=\mathbf 0 $$

i.e. solve for the direction $\mathbf d$ that $M$ collapses to zero, and stack a $0$ underneath it (the last coordinate $0$ marks a point on the plane at infinity). This singular case is elaborated in the next lecture.

worked example — camera centre from $M,\mathbf p_4$ (illustrative)

Say a solved camera has left block $M=I$ (identity, illustrative) and last column $\mathbf p_4=(2,-3,5)^{\top}$.

  1. $M^{-1}=I$, so $M^{-1}\mathbf p_4=(2,-3,5)^{\top}$.
  2. $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4=(-2,\ 3,\ -5)^{\top}$ — the camera sits at that world location.

the columns of $P$ are vanishing points

Write $P$ as four columns $[\,\mathbf p_1\ \mathbf p_2\ \mathbf p_3\ \mathbf p_4\,]$. A vanishing point is the image of a point at infinity along some direction $\mathbf d$ — all world lines parallel to $\mathbf d$ appear to converge there. A point at infinity is written $(\mathbf d;\,0)$ (last coordinate $0$). Feed the axis directions in:

$$ P\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf p_1,\quad P\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf p_2,\quad P\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf p_3,\quad P\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf p_4 $$
🧭 the four columns, decoded
  • $\mathbf p_1$ = vanishing point of the world X-axis
  • $\mathbf p_2$ = vanishing point of the world Y-axis
  • $\mathbf p_3$ = vanishing point of the world Z-axis
  • $\mathbf p_4$ = image of the world origin $(0,0,0)$ — since $(0,0,0,1)^{\top}$ picks out the last column

a vanishing point: parallel rails meet at the image of infinity

All lines sharing a direction $\mathbf d$ project to lines meeting at one image point — the vanishing point $P\,(\mathbf d;0)$. The three axis directions give the columns $\mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2,\mathbf p_3$.

$P = K[R\mid\mathbf t]$: intrinsics $K$ turn metres into pixels, extrinsics $R,\mathbf t$ place the camera.

$K$ = upper-triangular ($f_x,f_y,s,p_x,p_y$) · 11 DOF = 6 + 5 · 6 matches → DLT recovers $P$ · centre $=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$, columns = vanishing points.

    $K=\begin{bmatrix}f_x&s&p_x\\0&f_y&p_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$; $f_x=f\,m_x$ is the focal length in pixels along x, $p_x$ is the principal-point column (where the optical axis meets the sensor)
  2. A projective camera $P$ has how many degrees of freedom, and how do they split?
    11 total = 6 extrinsic (3 rotation + 3 translation) + 5 intrinsic ($f_x,f_y,s,p_x,p_y$)
  3. One point correspondence gives how many independent equations, and why not three?
    2 — the cross-product $\mathbf x_i\times P\mathbf X_i=\mathbf 0$ yields 3 rows, but one is a linear combination of the other two, so it's redundant; hence you need ≥ 6 matches
  4. Given $P=[\,M\mid\mathbf p_4\,]$ with $M$ non-singular, where is the camera centre?
    $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$, obtained from $P\mathbf C=\mathbf 0$

LECTURE 13

Camera Geometry — Part III

reading a camera out of its matrix P — centre, vanishing points, principal point, back-projection, depth
🧠 the big picture, first

The $3\times4$ matrix $P$ isn't just a projector — it's a treasure chest. Slice it the right way and it hands you the camera's centre, its viewing directions, its principal point, and the depth of any point — all by simple linear algebra.

This lecture is a toolbox of "given $P$, extract ___" recipes. Learn which slice of $P$ answers which question. 🔑

1 three ways to slice $P$

$$ P=[\,M\mid \mathbf p_4\,]=[\,\mathbf p_1\ \mathbf p_2\ \mathbf p_3\ \mathbf p_4\,]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf r_1^{T}\\ \mathbf r_2^{T}\\ \mathbf r_3^{T}\end{bmatrix} $$

$M$ = the left $3\times3$ block, $\mathbf p_4$ = the 4th column; $\mathbf p_i$ = columns; $\mathbf r_i^{T}$ = rows. Each slice reveals something different. 👇

2 the camera centre = the null-space of $P$

The camera centre $C$ is the one point with no image — you can't draw a ray from the centre to itself. Mathematically it's killed by $P$:

centre = right null vector
$$ P\,\mathbf C=\mathbf 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tilde{\mathbf C}=-\,M^{-1}\mathbf p_4 $$
read it as: plug $\mathbf C=(\tilde{\mathbf C},1)$ into $[M\mid\mathbf p_4]\mathbf C=\mathbf 0$ ⇒ $M\tilde{\mathbf C}+\mathbf p_4=\mathbf 0$ ⇒ $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$.

If $M$ is singular (not invertible), the centre lies at infinity — a direction, written $\mathbf C=(\mathbf d,0)$ found as the null vector of $M$ ($M\mathbf d=\mathbf 0$). That's an affine camera (next lecture).

🧮 the same thing by Cramer's rule (no explicit inverse)

Writing $P=[\,\mathbf p_1\ \mathbf p_2\ \mathbf p_3\ \mathbf p_4\,]$ by columns, the centre's coordinates are ratios of $3\times3$ column determinants:

$$ X=\det[\mathbf p_2\,\mathbf p_3\,\mathbf p_4],\ \ Y=-\det[\mathbf p_1\,\mathbf p_3\,\mathbf p_4],\ \ Z=\det[\mathbf p_1\,\mathbf p_2\,\mathbf p_4],\ \ T=-\det[\mathbf p_1\,\mathbf p_2\,\mathbf p_3] $$

giving $\mathbf C=(X,Y,Z,T)^{T}$ (de-homogenise by $T$). This is just Cramer's rule applied to $M\tilde{\mathbf C}=-\mathbf p_4$, and it works even when you'd rather not invert $M$.

3 the columns = vanishing points

Project a point at infinity along an axis (e.g. $\mathbf X=(1,0,0,0)^{T}$ for the X-direction). The result is just a column of $P$:

🧭 columns tell you where axes vanish
  • $\mathbf p_1$ = vanishing point of the X-axis (image of its point at infinity).
  • $\mathbf p_2$ = vanishing point of the Y-axis; $\ \mathbf p_3$ = vanishing point of the Z-axis.
  • $\mathbf p_4$ = image of the world origin $(0,0,0,1)^{T}$ (a real, finite point — scale is 1, not 0).

In general the vanishing point of any direction $\mathbf d$ is $M\mathbf d$ — only $M$ matters for vanishing points (the translation $\mathbf p_4$ drops out).

4 the rows = planes; the principal point

Each row $\mathbf r_i^{T}$ defines a plane $\mathbf r_i^{T}\mathbf X=0$ of world points that image onto a special image line:

  • $\mathbf r_3^{T}\mathbf X=0$ — the principal plane (parallel to the image plane, through the centre; its points image at infinity, scale = 0).
  • $\mathbf r_1^{T}\mathbf X=0$ and $\mathbf r_2^{T}\mathbf X=0$ — the two axis planes (imaged onto the image $Y$- and $X$-axes).

Let $\mathbf m_3^{T}$ be the third row of $M$ (i.e. $(p_{31},p_{32},p_{33})$) — it's the normal direction of the principal plane, so it points along the principal ray. The principal point is its image:

$$ \mathbf x_0=M\,\mathbf m_3,\qquad \text{principal-ray direction}=\det(M)\,\mathbf m_3 $$

(Multiplying by $\det(M)$ fixes the sign so the ray points the right way — towards the scene, not behind the camera.)

5 back-projection & depth

A pixel $\mathbf x$ came from a whole ray. That ray passes through the centre $\tilde{\mathbf C}$ in the direction $M^{-1}\mathbf x$, so every world point on it is:

back-projected ray
$$ \tilde{\mathbf X}(\lambda)=\tilde{\mathbf C}+\lambda\,M^{-1}\mathbf x $$

To get a single point you need one more constraint (a known plane, or a known distance). And the depth of a scene point $\tilde{\mathbf X}$ is its distance along the principal axis — the projection onto the unit principal-ray direction:

$$ \text{depth}=\hat{\mathbf m}_3^{T}\big(\tilde{\mathbf X}-\tilde{\mathbf C}\big) $$

6 recovering K, R, C from P

Since $M=KR$ with $K$ upper-triangular and $R$ orthogonal, this is exactly what an RQ decomposition produces:

🧩 the recipe
  1. RQ-decompose $M \to$ upper-triangular $\times$ orthogonal $= K\,R$. Read the 5 intrinsics off $K$, the rotation off $R$.
  2. Camera centre $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$; translation $\mathbf t=-R\tilde{\mathbf C}$.
  3. Fix signs so $K$ has positive focal lengths (the decomposition is unique up to that sign convention).

$P$ is a treasure chest: centre = its null-space, columns = vanishing points, rows = planes, $M=KR$.

$\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$; $\mathbf p_{1,2,3}$ = axis vanishing points; principal point $=M\mathbf m_3$; decompose with RQ.

  1. How do you find the camera centre from $P=[M\mid\mathbf p_4]$?
    it's the null-space of $P$: $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$
  2. What does the 4th column $\mathbf p_4$ of $P$ represent?
    the image of the world origin $(0,0,0,1)^{T}$
  3. Which row of $P$ gives the principal plane, and what's special about it?
    the third row: $\mathbf r_3^{T}\mathbf X=0$ — the plane through the centre parallel to the image plane
  4. How are the intrinsics $K$ and rotation $R$ recovered from $P$?
    RQ-decompose the left block $M=KR$ ($K$ upper-triangular, $R$ orthogonal)

LECTURE 14

Camera Geometry — Part IV

worked problems on P · the affine camera · imaging a plane/line · views from a fixed centre (rotation & zoom)
🧠 the big picture, first

Two halves today. First we practise the "read-off-$P$" recipes from Lecture 13 on real problems (exam style!). Then we meet two important special cases: the affine camera and what happens when the camera only rotates or zooms — which quietly brings back the homography.

1 worked problem — extracting geometry from $P$

given a projection matrix $P=[\,M\mid\mathbf p_4\,]$, find…
  1. Camera centre: $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$ (invert $M$ via cofactors/determinant). Leave it in world 3-D coords — don't "de-homogenise".
  2. Vanishing point of the X-axis: $P\,(1,0,0,0)^{T}=\mathbf p_1$ (the first column of $P$). To read it as an image point, divide by its last coordinate. (e.g. $\mathbf p_1=(a,b,c)^{T}\to(a/c,\,b/c)$ — illustrative)
  3. Image of the origin: $P\,(0,0,0,1)^{T}=\mathbf p_4$. His example gives $(1,1,1)^{T}\to(1,1)$.
  4. Vanishing point of a direction with direction-cosines $(4\!:\!3\!:\!2)$: $P\,(4,3,2,0)^{T}=M(4,3,2)^{T}$, then divide by the last coordinate to read the image point.

Rule of thumb: direction (scale 0) ⇒ vanishing point; finite point (scale 1) ⇒ ordinary image point.

2 worked problem — back-projecting to a known distance

image point $(2,7)$ is 40 units from the centre — find the 3-D point
  1. Camera centre: $\tilde{\mathbf C}=-M^{-1}\mathbf p_4$.
  2. Ray direction: $\mathbf d=M^{-1}(2,7,1)^{T}$ (back-project the homogeneous pixel).
  3. Walk 40 units along the unit direction: $\ \tilde{\mathbf X}=\tilde{\mathbf C}+40\,\dfrac{\mathbf d}{\lVert\mathbf d\rVert}$.

A single image gives a ray; the extra "distance = 40" constraint picks the one point on it. 🎯

📐 bonus recipes on the same $P$
  • Principal plane = last row: $\mathbf r_3^{T}\mathbf X=0$, e.g. $(1,-5,8,1)\Rightarrow x-5y+8z+1=0$.
  • Dihedral angle between planes $x_1Ox_2$ and $x_3Ox_4$ (through the centre $O$): each plane is $\boldsymbol\pi=P^{T}(\mathbf x_i\times\mathbf x_j)$; take unit normals, $\cos^{-1}$ of their dot product. His answer: $\approx 53.75^{\circ}$.
  • Shortcut for a camera at the origin (canonical, $f=1$, no $P$ given): a homogeneous image point $\mathbf x=(x,y,1)^{T}$ is the direction of its ray from $O$. So the plane through $O$ and two image points $\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2$ has normal $\ \mathbf n=\mathbf x_1\times\mathbf x_2$, and the unit normal is $\mathbf n/\lVert\mathbf n\rVert$. (This is $\boldsymbol\pi=P^{T}(\mathbf x_1\times\mathbf x_2)$ with $P^{T}\to I$, and it's what you use when only image points — not $P$ or $K$ — are given.)

3 the affine camera (centre at infinity)

What if $M$ is singular? Then the centre sits at infinity and the camera projects with parallel rays instead of converging ones — an affine camera. Canonically its last row is $(0,0,0,1)$.

🧊 properties of the affine camera
  • Its principal plane is the plane at infinity, and the centre lies on it.
  • Points at infinity map to points at infinity ⇒ parallel lines stay parallel in the image (no vanishing points).
  • The relation simplifies to plain Euclidean coordinates: $\ \tilde{\mathbf x}=M_{2\times3}\,\tilde{\mathbf X}+\mathbf t$ (no divide-by-$Z$!).
  • 8 DOF ($M_{2\times3}$ has 6, $\mathbf t$ has 2) — so 4 point correspondences suffice; more points ⇒ linear least-squares. ($M_{2\times3}$ must be rank 2.)

A good model when the object is small & far (weak perspective) — the maths gets much friendlier.

4 imaging a plane = a homography

Put a scene plane at $Z=0$ (always possible by choosing coordinates). Then a point on it is $(X,Y,0,1)^{T}$, and $P\mathbf X$ drops its 3rd column:

$$ \mathbf x=[\,\mathbf p_1\ \mathbf p_2\ \mathbf p_4\,]\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\1\end{bmatrix}=H_{3\times3}\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\1\end{bmatrix} $$
key takeaway: the perspective image of a plane is a $3\times3$ homography between the plane and the image — exactly the map from Week 2. That's why rectifying a planar signboard worked.

5 imaging a 3-D line

A 3-D line images to a 2-D line $\mathbf l$. The camera centre and the 3-D line span a plane, and that plane is simply:

$$ \boldsymbol\pi=P^{T}\mathbf l $$

Proof in one line: image points satisfy $\mathbf l^{T}\mathbf x=0=\mathbf l^{T}P\mathbf X=(P^{T}\mathbf l)^{T}\mathbf X$ — a point-on-plane relation with plane $P^{T}\mathbf l$.

6 two views from the same centre — rotation & zoom

If the camera stays put ($\tilde{\mathbf C}$ fixed) but rotates or changes intrinsics, the two images of any scene are related by a homography — regardless of depth:

same-centre homography
$$ \mathbf x_2=H\,\mathbf x_1,\qquad H=K_2R_2\,(K_1R_1)^{-1} $$

Special case — pure zoom (no rotation, image planes parallel, focal-length ratio $k$): the calibration just scales,

$$ K_2=K_1\,\operatorname{diag}(k,k,1),\qquad H=K_2K_1^{-1} $$

so zooming multiplies displacements from the principal point by $k$ — a similarity about the principal point. This is the geometry behind panorama stitching (rotate-only) and digital zoom.

read $P$ for centre/vanishing-points/planes; parallel rays ⇒ affine camera; a plane images as a homography.

image of a plane = 3×3 H; image of a line's plane = $P^{T}\mathbf l$; rotate/zoom about a fixed centre = homography $K_2R_2(K_1R_1)^{-1}$.

  1. You project the point at infinity $(4,3,2,0)^{T}$. Is the result a vanishing point or an ordinary image point, and how do you read it off?
    a vanishing point ($=M(4,3,2)^{T}$); divide by its last coordinate to get the 2-D image location
  2. What geometric feature makes a camera "affine", and what does it do to parallel lines?
    its centre is at infinity (M singular, last row $(0,0,0,1)$) → parallel projection → parallel lines stay parallel (no vanishing points)
  3. The image of a planar scene is what kind of transformation?
    a $3\times3$ homography between the scene plane and the image
  4. Two photos from the same centre (camera only rotates) are related how?
    by a homography $H=K_2R_2(K_1R_1)^{-1}$ — independent of scene depth (basis of panorama stitching)

LECTURE 15

Camera Geometry — Part V

conjugate-rotation homography · vanishing points & lines · panorama mosaicing · single-view wrap-up
🧠 the big picture, first

We close single-view geometry with the payoffs: rotating a camera gives a special homography that literally encodes the rotation angle; vanishing points let us recover directions and rotations; and stitching it all together builds panoramas.

1 the conjugate-rotation homography

Fix the camera centre and only rotate it by $R$ (intrinsics $K$ unchanged). Then $P_1=K[I\mid\mathbf 0]$ and $P_2=K[R\mid\mathbf 0]$, and the two images relate by:

rotation homography
$$ \mathbf x'=H\mathbf x,\qquad H=K\,R\,K^{-1} $$

Because $H$ is similar to the rotation $R$, it shares $R$'s eigenvalues:

$$ \text{eig}(H)=\big\{\,\mu,\ \mu e^{\,i\theta},\ \mu e^{-i\theta}\,\big\} $$
🌀 two gifts from this "conjugate rotation"
  • The complex eigenvalues give the rotation angle $\theta$ directly — you can measure how much the camera turned between two shots.
  • The eigenvector of the real eigenvalue is the vanishing point of the rotation axis.

2 two applications: rectification & mosaicing

🖼️ synthetic fronto-parallel views

Photograph an oblique planar surface, pick target corners with the true aspect ratio, compute the homography, and warp → a straightened, fronto-parallel view (rectification, as in Week 2).

🌄 panoramic mosaicing

A camera with a narrow field of view rotates to capture a wide scene. Each view relates to a reference by a homography $H_i$; warp them all onto one reference plane and blend → a single panorama. (Rotation-only ⇒ depth doesn't matter.)

3 vanishing points, properly

A vanishing point is the image of a point at infinity — geometrically, where the line through the camera centre parallel to a scene line $L$ meets the image plane. All lines in direction $\mathbf d$ share one vanishing point:

vanishing point of direction d
$$ \mathbf v=K\mathbf d\quad(\text{no rotation}),\qquad \mathbf v=KR\,\mathbf d\quad(\text{rotated camera}) $$
read it as: a vanishing point depends only on direction + $K$ (+ rotation) — not on the camera's position. Translate the camera and vanishing points stay put. 🌙 That's why the moon seems to follow you as you walk: pure translation, so its (near-infinite) direction — and thus its image position — doesn't change.

Payoff: if you can measure vanishing points (intersect images of parallel lines) and you know $K$, then $\mathbf d\propto K^{-1}\mathbf v$ recovers 3-D directions — and two such directions constrain the rotation $R$ (it's orthonormal + has one angle).

4 the vanishing line of a plane (the horizon)

Every vanishing point of every line lying in a plane $\pi$ falls on a single image line — the vanishing line (the plane's horizon). Geometrically it's where the plane through the camera centre parallel to $\pi$ meets the image plane.

The 3-D plane through the centre and an image line $\mathbf l$ is $\boldsymbol\pi=P^{T}\mathbf l$; its first three entries are the plane normal. For a canonical camera $K[I\mid0]$ the normal direction is simply $K^{T}\mathbf l$.

worked exercise — normal of a plane from its horizon

A line images as $3x+4y=5$, i.e. $\mathbf l=(3,4,-5)^{T}$, and it is the horizon of some plane. Find that plane's normal.

  1. Plane through centre & line: $\boldsymbol\pi=P^{T}\mathbf l$ → (professor's numbers) $7x+4y+8\ldots z+\ldots=0$.
  2. The normal is the first three components of $\boldsymbol\pi$; normalise to a unit vector. ✅

To get a vanishing line in practice: take several sets of parallel lines in the plane, find each set's vanishing point, and join them.
